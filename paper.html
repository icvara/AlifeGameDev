Simulated nature, unstudied

Artificial life games and the gap in research on games and conservation.

ALife Game Dev community. A short, open draft meant to start a discussion, not a peer-reviewed article. Every number in it is computed live from the data on this site, so it updates when games are added or scores corrected. Comments and corrections are welcome through the survey.

Abstract

Artificial life games, where creatures live, evolve and form ecosystems on their own, have rarely been studied as a group. This paper does three things. (1) Evaluation. We propose two grids to measure how much life a game simulates: an ALife score (0–12) built from the ingredients of artificial life research, and a Life score (0–14) built from the seven characteristics of life in biology. Across games they agree closely (r = ). (2) Description. Using explicit inclusion rules, we describe artificial life games: a genre that faded after the 1990s and returned strongly after 2015, built on autonomy and emergence but rarely on learning brains, and played by small audiences (median Steam reviews). Mainstream genres built around living things, from colony sims to creature collectors, simulate far less: only of well-known games reach the artificial life threshold. (3) Ecology. Adding an environmental score (0–8) based on the STRATEGIES report Green Transition at Play, we compare artificial life games with the games most studied in conservation research (Blake et al.'s systematic map). Simulating life and engaging with nature turn out to be independent (r = ). The studied games average /12 on the ALife score, against /12 for artificial life games, while both sit close together on the environmental score ( and /8). Research has looked at games that show nature and largely missed the games that simulate it; very few games do both, which points to a research gap and an open design space.

1. Introduction

Games are increasingly discussed as a way to engage people with ecology and climate. A recent systematic map found 380 papers covering 450 commercial games and biodiversity, but also noted how few of them measure effects with pre/post tests or control groups, and that there is still no evidence on whether games change real-world pro-ecological behaviour (Blake et al., 2026).

Meanwhile, a separate tradition has built games around artificial life (ALife): creatures with their own drives, genomes that mutate and are selected, food webs with feedback, and outcomes that nobody scripted (Langton, 1989). From Creatures (Grand et al., 1997) to SimLife, SimEarth and recent indie titles, these games ask players to work with living systems rather than look at them.

1.1 Soft artificial life: a short state of the art

Artificial life studies "life as it could be" by building it, in software ("soft" ALife), hardware or chemistry (Langton, 1989). Its software branch starts with von Neumann's self-reproducing automata (von Neumann, 1966) and Conway's Game of Life, which showed how complex, lifelike patterns can emerge from a few local rules (Gardner, 1970). The field took its name at the first ALife workshop in 1987.

Three lines of work matter most for games. Digital evolution lets self-replicating programs compete for resources: in Tierra, parasites and hosts emerged on their own (Ray, 1991), and Avida has been used to test evolutionary theory directly, showing how complex features can evolve step by step (Lenski et al., 2003). Evolved bodies and brains began with Sims' virtual creatures, whose morphology and neural control evolved together (Sims, 1994), and grew with neuroevolution methods such as NEAT (Stanley & Miikkulainen, 2002), which were soon used in games like NERO and Galactic Arms Race. Self-organisation has seen a recent revival: continuous cellular automata like Lenia produce a zoo of moving, lifelike forms (Chan, 2019), neural cellular automata learn to grow and repair shapes (Mordvintsev et al., 2020), and computer-designed "xenobots" built from frog cells carried the field into living matter (Kriegman et al., 2020).

The central open problem is open-endedness: no artificial system yet keeps producing genuine novelty indefinitely the way biological evolution does (Bedau et al., 2000; Packard et al., 2019). Ideas like novelty search, which rewards being different rather than being better (Lehman & Stanley, 2011), and, very recently, the use of foundation models to search the space of possible simulations for lifelike behaviour (Kumar et al., 2024), are attempts to get there. Games are an interesting test bed: they put these systems in front of millions of people, and players are very good at noticing when a world stops surprising them.

1.2 Aims and structure

This paper has three aims, which give it its structure. First, how can we evaluate how much artificial life a game contains? We propose two scoring grids and check that they agree (section 3). Second, what do artificial life games look like? We describe them as a genre: when they were made, which ingredients of life they actually simulate, how many people play them, and how far mainstream genres built around living things are from them (section 4). Third, how do they relate to ecology games and to research on games and nature? We add an environmental score and ask whether the games studied for their relationship with nature actually simulate nature (section 5).

2.1 Sources

Two sources were combined. 2.2 Inclusion rules

The rules were fixed before the analysis and are applied in this order, by the page itself, so anyone can check or change them:

  1. R1, released. The game must be publicly available. Announced and early-development titles are excluded, since they cannot be played or studied yet.
  2. R2, not a prototype. itch.io entries are excluded: most are prototypes, jam games or unfinished experiments, which would not be comparable with commercial titles.
  3. R3, video games. Board games are excluded from the main comparison and reported separately (section 5.7), because they cannot run a simulation on their own.
  4. R4, actually artificial life. A game counts as an artificial life game if it scores at least /12 on the ALife score, whichever list of the site it comes from: games filed under "Related" or "Play as an animal" that reach the threshold join the artificial life group. Games from the site's artificial life lists (Steam, elsewhere, projects) that score below it are excluded, since they merely touch on the theme (cosy breeding sims, aquarium managers, pure cellular automata). The two comparison samples, popular Steam games and conservation research games, are kept whole whatever their scores, so they stay faithful to how they were defined.

Games from Blake et al.'s map that were already in the catalogue (, such as Eco, SimEarth and Subnautica) stay in their catalogue group and join the conservation research group, which therefore holds all most-studied games. These games are counted in both groups; in the figures they get their own mark (a dark diamond when they are also artificial life games, an orange ring when they are also popular Steam games).

    2.3 Scoring

    Every game is scored on the scales defined in section 3 (artificial life) and section 5.1 (environment). Scores were assigned by the site's editors, with AI assistance, from store pages, official descriptions and developer material. They are rough estimates, not measurements (see section 7).

    3. How to evaluate artificial life in games

    To compare games, we need a way to say how much "life" a game simulates. We use two independent grids: an ALife score built from the ingredients of artificial life research, and a Life score built from the characteristics of life used in biology. Both score each criterion 0 (absent), 1 (partly, or steered by the player) or 2 (fully, on its own).

    3.1 The ALife score

    ALife score (0–12). Six criteria scored 0, 1 or 2: autonomy (creatures act on their own drives), brains (behaviour from learning or neural networks), body (body organisation that varies or can be built), evolution (inheritance with variation; 2 when natural selection decides), ecosystem (species and resources depend on each other with feedback) and emergence (unscripted outcomes).

    The six criteria follow the three lines of research of section 1.1: autonomy and emergence come from self-organisation, brains and body from evolved bodies and brains, evolution and ecosystem from digital evolution. A game reaching /12 or more is treated as an artificial life game (rule R4).

    3.2 The Life score

    Life score (0–14). The ALife score is our own design, so we also scored games against an independent and much older yardstick: the seven characteristics of life used in biology textbooks. These are organisation (internal structure), metabolism (taking in and using energy), homeostasis (regulating an internal state), growth, reproduction, response to stimuli and adaptation over generations. Each is scored 0, 1 or 2 for any living thing the game simulates, including the player's avatar when its biology is simulated (hunger, body temperature). Definitions of life are themselves debated (Koshland, 2002), so this list is a convenient common reference rather than a definitive one. It was scored for every game in the three main categories of the comparison, including artificial life games below the ALife threshold and studied games listed elsewhere on the site: artificial life games, conservation research games and popular Steam games.

    4. Describing artificial life games

    We first describe the artificial life games that passed the inclusion rules: on Steam, released elsewhere (consoles, classic PC, mobile, browser), research projects and software toys, and games the site files under other lists but which pass the threshold (). To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to characterise artificial life games as a genre with a common scoring grid.

    6. Discussion

    Why the gap matters. Ecological understanding is largely about systems: feedback loops, delays, carrying capacity, coevolution and collapse (Meadows, 2008). A beautiful forest teaches what nature looks like; a population that crashes because you removed its predator teaches how nature works. Artificial life games are, by construction, interactive models of those dynamics. In STRATEGIES' terms, they are strong on plasticity (worlds that change) and on mechanics where "the environment touches back", the very properties the report highlights in games like Rain World.

    Why they may have been missed. Most artificial life games are small and independent, released on itch.io or in early access, with modest audiences (the median artificial life game on Steam has reviews, and the deepest simulations have the fewest players; section 4.3), while conservation research has tended to follow large player bases (Pokémon GO, Animal Crossing, Minecraft). They rarely present themselves as environmental games, so they do not appear in searches for "eco" or "climate" games. And their subject, evolution and emergence, sits in a different academic community (artificial life, complex systems) from game studies and conservation.

    A research agenda. We suggest:

    1. Study systemic games directly. Compare players of a living-system game (e.g. Equilinox, Eco, a predator–prey sandbox) with players of a scenic nature game on systems-thinking measures, with pre/post tests and a control group, as Blake et al. recommend.
    2. Look at mechanics, not only representation. Ecocritical readings focus on what a world depicts; artificial life games ask what a world does when you intervene.
    3. Use the overlap as a design brief. The near-empty corner of Figure 9, high on both scales, is an invitation to developers: simulated ecosystems with a clear environmental intention.
    4. Bring the communities together. ALife researchers, game developers and conservation scientists rarely meet. Shared datasets like Blake et al.'s map, and community efforts like this site, can help.

    7. Limitations

    The scores are editorial estimates from public descriptions, not long play sessions, and they were not checked by independent raters, so no inter-rater reliability is available. Both scales are our own operationalisations: the STRATEGIES report presents its points of inquiry as questions, not as a scoring grid. "Unknown" counts as 0, which lowers environmental totals, especially for impact. The inclusion rules are applied systematically, but the catalogue they are applied to was built by curation and community suggestions, not by a database search with a fixed query, so some artificial life games are surely missing. The ALife threshold (≥ /12) is a choice: it is set as one constant in the page's code, so its effect can be tested. Excluding itch.io also removes some genuinely innovative work. The research group uses only the most-studied games in Blake et al.'s map, not all 450. The comparison is descriptive: it shows where attention has gone, not why.

    8. Conclusion

    The games researchers have chosen to understand how play relates to nature mostly picture nature. The games that simulate it, where creatures live, compete, evolve and collapse on their own, have been almost entirely left out. Closing that gap would give conservation research a new kind of evidence, and give artificial life game developers a new reason to design for the planet.
