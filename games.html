Artificial life games

Games where part of the design is handed over to a simulation: creatures that evolve, breed, learn, or live in ecosystems of their own. Plus the simulations and research projects that inspired them.

How the ALife score works

A rough estimate of how much artificial life each game actually simulates. It's not a quality rating: a great game can score low. Six criteria, each scored 0, 1 or 2, for a total out of 12:

    Scores are based on what each game says it simulates (store pages, websites, devlogs), not on long play sessions, so some will be off. Hover a score to see the breakdown, and suggest corrections in the survey. See the scores as charts →

    How the environmental score works

    A second, separate score: how much a game engages with the natural environment and sustainability. It follows the four "points of inquiry" for sustainability games in the STRATEGIES report Green Transition at Play (Fizek et al., 2025, section 2.2). Each criterion is scored 0, 1 or 2, for a total out of 8:

      A ? means we found no information, and it counts as 0 in the total. Impact is almost always unknown: the report itself notes there is no reliable way to measure it yet. Like the ALife score, it is a rough estimate, not a quality rating.

                Play as an animal

                Games about living one animal's life inside a wild ecosystem: hunting, growing up, raising young, surviving. Usually more about nature than about simulated life. Saurian, in the Steam list, belongs here too. Newest first.

                  Board games

                  Tabletop games about evolution, genetics, ecosystems and the environment, scored on both scales. Their ALife scores stay low by nature: the players move everything, so nothing is autonomous and there are no brains, but traits, food webs and emergent play still count. Highest environmental score first. The STRATEGIES report covers the board game industry too.
